---
title: "Missing the Bottom Rung"
date: 2026-01-22
excerpt: "Why getting stuck on hard technical topics is rarely a lack of intelligence, and how unstated foundational steps make us feel worse than we are."
tags: ["learning", "cognition", "craft"]
---

For a long time, I believed that some fields were simply meant for a smarter kind of person. In my first year of university, sitting in big lecture rooms looking at complicated heat equations and chemical flow charts, my brain would freeze. The teacher moved quickly across the board, solving equations like it was the most obvious thing in the world, while I sat staring at the symbols feeling completely lost. When everyone around you seems to nod and take notes with ease, it is hard not to blame yourself. You tell yourself that your brain is just not built for heavy math, and that other people were born with faster engines.

When I switched over to computer science, I thought things would get clearer, but the exact same feeling was waiting for me. The first time I tried to understand pointers, memory addresses, and how programs run at the same time without breaking, my head hurt in the exact same way. Reading about how data moves across networks felt like watching someone play complex jazz while I was still trying to find the basic notes on the piano. It took me a long time to realize that this feeling had nothing to do with how smart I was.

When a hard subject feels like a brick wall, the problem is almost always a missing step that nobody bothered to explain. When a professor or a textbook writer calls something basic, they often forget that they spent ten years getting used to the ideas behind it. They can jump from step one straight to step five because steps two, three, and four have become second nature to them. When you get stuck at step two, you rarely realize that you are just missing a small, unstated detail about how memory works or how equations are set up. You just assume you are bad at the whole field.

Once you realize this, getting stuck stops feeling like a personal failure and starts looking like a clue. Getting lost in a chemistry problem usually means your basic calculus was a bit rusty. Struggling to fix a strange bug in code usually means you have not looked closely at how the operating system handles tasks behind the scenes. Getting stuck on a hard paper almost always means the writer is using terms from another field that you have not seen yet. Going backward to fill in what you missed feels slow, but once you find the missing piece, the hard idea suddenly looks simple and normal.

I still run into things that confuse me completely on the first try. The only difference is that feeling lost does not make me panic anymore. Instead of thinking that a topic is beyond me, I just ask what simple thing I was supposed to know before I started reading. Doing well in hard subjects is rarely about having a genius brain. It is mostly about climbing the ladder one step at a time without skipping the bottom rungs.
