---
title: "The Romans Also Had Bad Days"
date: 2026-03-14
excerpt: "History books compress centuries into dramatic turning points, blinding us to the reality that human life has always been lived in the messy, unheroic present."
tags: ["history", "perspective", "philosophy"]
---

Whenever you read history, you are looking at a timelapse of a mountain eroding. You read a chapter titled *The Fall of the Western Roman Empire*, and within twenty-five pages, three centuries of politics, migration, currency debasement, and institutional decay are wrapped into a tidy narrative arc. It feels like watching an inevitable tragedy where every general knew his cue and every emperor was acutely aware of his historical legacy.

We tend to read the past with a dramatic soundtrack running in our heads. We picture historical figures pacing along stone ramparts in dramatic tunics, staring into the sunset with the burden of civilization weighing upon their furrowed brows. We rarely imagine them having a toothache. We don't imagine a Roman clerk in 410 AD waking up on a Tuesday with a stiff neck, annoyed because his roommate drank the last of the wine, trudging to a dreary municipal office to audit tax receipts while wondering if he can afford a new pair of leather sandals. Yet that mundane reality is what ninety-nine percent of human history actually was.

The fundamental flaw of historical memory is compression. When we look backward, our brains discard the boring days—the three hundred consecutive afternoons where nothing happened except laundry, small trade disputes, gossip at market stalls, and light rain. We only preserve the assassinations, the treaties, the volcanic eruptions, and the sackings of cities. Because of this, we convince ourselves that past eras were grander, more purposeful, and heavier with meaning than our own. We look at our messy, confusing modern lives—our video calls, grocery runs, awkward text messages, and late-night scrolling—and feel an odd sense of historical inadequacy, thinking those people were living through History while we are just living through an ordinary Tuesday.

No one ever felt like they were living inside a textbook chapter while it was happening. The people who lived through the Renaissance did not wake up in Florence to whisper about historical epochs; they complained about the price of eggs, worried about localized fevers, argued with relatives over inheritance, and hoped their shoes would not leak in the winter mud.

If you want a grounded view of the ancient world, look past formal stone decrees to the graffiti scratched into the plaster of Pompeii's taverns. One patron wrote, *"Gaius Pumidius Diphilus was here on the fifth day before the nones of October."* Another scratched, *"The weaver Successus loves the innkeeper's slave girl named Iris; she does not care for him, but he still begs."* On a different wall, someone scrawled, *"O wall, I am amazed you have not collapsed under the weight of so many boring writers."* These were ordinary people sharing unrequited crushes, petty gripes, inside jokes, and silly rivalries, inhabiting the same quiet banality we navigate today.

There is steadying perspective in remembering that every era was lived forward without a map, minute by minute, by people mostly trying to get through the afternoon. When we look at the turbulence of our current era—economic shifts, political polarization, rapid technological changes—it is easy to imagine that the past was a stable, coherent landscape. Yet the past was just as foggy, uncertain, and contradictory to the people experiencing it. Marcus Aurelius wrote his private journal at the freezing northern frontier of the Danube largely to keep himself from losing his temper with court officials the next morning.

Whenever daily life feels too small or disorganized against the backdrop of history, remember the municipal clerk in Rome, the weaver in Pompeii, and the emperor in his tent. None of them knew how their century would be summarized in a textbook index. The practical task has always been to navigate the next hour with whatever quiet decency you can muster.
