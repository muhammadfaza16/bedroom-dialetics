---
title: "The Curse of Having Taste Before Having Skill"
date: 2026-07-19
excerpt: "Why your early creations feel disappointing, and why that quiet dissatisfaction is the only proof that you actually know what good looks like."
tags: ["craft", "taste", "creation"]
---

There is a particular kind of misery unique to beginners who have good taste. You know what great prose sounds like. You can spot an immaculate typography hierarchy from across the room. You know when a piece of software feels weightless, and you know when an argument lands with devastating clarity.

Then you sit down to create something of your own. You write three paragraphs, look at the screen, and feel an immediate wave of revulsion. It looks clunky, the rhythm is off, the sentences are clumsy, and the gap between what was in your head and what landed on the page feels like an ocean. Most people quit here, concluding that because their initial output is mediocre, they simply lack the gift.

That painful dissonance occurs because critical taste develops years before mechanical execution. Your taste is what drew you to the medium in the first place, and your taste is what makes you ambitious. But your taste is also the exact standard that makes you dissatisfied with your early attempts. If you lacked taste, you would produce something mediocre and feel thrilled with it, blind to its rough edges. Disliking your early work is evidence that your perceptual radar is functioning well ahead of your current motor skill.

The remedy for this tension is volume without premature judgment. You have to accept producing work that sits below your own standards for a while. You might need twenty clumsy essays to arrive at one that feels honest, or twelve awkward interfaces to understand why the thirteenth functions cleanly. Early on, the task is simply to narrow the distance between what you can perceive and what you can build, one draft at a time.
