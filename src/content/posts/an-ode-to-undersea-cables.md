---
title: "An Ode to Undersea Cables"
date: 2026-01-08
excerpt: "We worship founders and disruptors who build apps in the cloud, but modern civilization is actually held together by tired engineers fixing glass fibers at the bottom of the ocean."
tags: ["technology", "history", "infrastructure"]
---

We have spent the last two decades convincing ourselves that the modern world is weightless.

We talk about "the cloud" as if our data lives in a celestial ether, floating peacefully above the messy earth. We send high-resolution video streams across continents in a hundred milliseconds, tap glass screens to summon cars, and trade digital assets generated by mathematical proofs. Because the interfaces are sleek, white, and frictionless, we easily forget that the digital world remains a physical, heavy, and vulnerable industrial machine anchored into the mud of the ocean floor.

Right now, roughly ninety-nine percent of all intercontinental internet traffic does not travel through satellites. It travels through roughly five hundred fiber-optic cables resting on the pitch-black floor of the world's oceans. These cables are not massive concrete tunnels. The actual data-carrying core is a bundle of glass strands barely thicker than a human hair, wrapped in layers of petroleum jelly, copper sheathing, steel wire armor, and high-density polyethylene.

At the bottom of the sea, thousands of meters below sunlight, in total darkness and crushing hydrostatic pressure, the thoughts, financial settlements, hospital records, and late-night voice notes of the human race travel as pulses of light bouncing through glass. Every week, somewhere on earth, a submarine cable gets bitten by a shark, snagged by a commercial fishing trawler’s anchor, or snapped by an underwater earthquake.

When that happens, continuity depends entirely on a crew of tired, salt-crusted sailors on a specialized cable ship rolling in fifteen-foot waves in the North Atlantic. They lower a grapple hook to drag a broken glass wire from the abyss, splice hair-thin fibers together in a clean room under a microscope while the ship lurches in a squall, and lower it back into the silt.

We live in a culture that suffers from a severe psychological bias toward the creator and a profound blindness toward the maintainer. We write biographical hagiographies about tech founders who introduce new social platforms or sleek consumer gadgets, giving keynote speeches about disruption, exponential scale, and moving fast.

Yet almost nothing that makes daily human life bearable was built this morning. The sewage pipes under your street that keep cholera away were laid forty years ago. The high-voltage power grid running your refrigerator was engineered by people who have been dead for three decades. The concrete bridges you drive over were poured by anonymous laborers working night shifts in the rain. Civilization does not advance merely because visionary thinkers dream up new futures. Civilization survives because a vast army of invisible technicians wakes up before dawn to inspect boiler pressure gauges, replace faulty track switches on commuter lines, patch asphalt, and clean water filtration membranes.

When you spend all day writing code, answering emails, or managing digital spreadsheets, it is dangerously easy to become alienated from reality, believing that human problems can all be resolved with software patches and elegant prose. But physical infrastructure is a quiet cure for intellectual arrogance. It reminds you that reality has physical teeth. Steel rusts, concrete cracks under load, glass fractures, and gravity operates without pause.

The next time you load a webpage in a fraction of a second, pause for a moment and look out the window toward the horizon. Picture the cold ocean miles away, the dark silt of the continental shelf, and the thin glass thread carrying the light of human consciousness through the deep water, quietly tended by people whose names you will never know.
