---
title: "The Quiet Relief of Having No Opinion"
date: 2026-06-25
excerpt: "We are conditioned to have immediate takes on everything within five minutes. Here is the case for leaving thoughts halfway unformed."
tags: ["philosophy", "culture", "attention"]
---

Online culture places a constant premium on immediate, high-conviction reactions to recent events. A controversy erupts, a new tool is released, or a public figure makes an error, and within the hour, feeds fill with definitive commentary and tribal declarations. Taking time to think is easily mistaken for disinterest.

Deciding what you think too quickly turns you into a defender of your first impression. Your pride becomes tied to an initial verdict, fresh evidence that complicates your stance feels unwelcome, and you expend energy defending an opinion formed in thirty seconds before understanding the underlying mechanics. Most meaningful questions in technology, craft, relationships, and ethics are inherently messy and resist quick summaries.

Admitting that you have not thought about an issue enough to hold a firm view relieves unnecessary pressure. It acknowledges that many subjects require sustained attention, and that no one is obligated to issue verdicts on every unfolding event. You are free to let an idea simmer quietly for months, or to recognize that certain debates simply do not require your judgment. Leaving the impulse to adjudicate behind brings a steady, practical calm.
